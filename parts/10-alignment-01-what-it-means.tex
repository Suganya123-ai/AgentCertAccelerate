\section{From a wrong answer to a wrong action}

The first AI systems most companies deployed were chatbots. You typed a question, the model answered, and if it got the answer wrong, you laughed, complained to IT, and moved on. The blast radius of a bad answer was a customer hangup. The category of system you were running was \textit{advisory}: the model spoke, a human acted.

That changes the moment you give the system a tool. The first time it can update a ticket, refund a charge, or read a row from a database, you have moved from advisory intelligence to \textit{operational autonomy}. Yesterday a hallucination was embarrassing. Today a hallucination is a wire transfer to the wrong account.

This is what people mean by \textbf{agent}. The shorthand on a vendor slide makes it sound like an upgrade to a chatbot. The reality on the ground is that you have a new category of system on your hands, with a new category of failure mode.

\begin{TNdefinition}{Agent} a software system that takes a goal in natural language, decides on its own which steps to take, calls tools to do the work, and adjusts based on what comes back. What distinguishes an agent from a chatbot is that it acts. \end{TNdefinition}

\begin{TNdefinition}{Agent loop} the repeating cycle of ``model produces a response, the response triggers a tool call, the tool returns a result, the model reads the result, the model produces the next response.'' Most agent failure modes are a particular thing going wrong in a particular leg of this loop. \end{TNdefinition}

\begin{TNdefinition}{Tool call} the atomic act of an agent invoking a function, API, or service to do something in the world: search a database, send an email, refund a charge. The line between advisory intelligence and operational autonomy is the first tool call. \end{TNdefinition}

The shorthand for the cost of a wrong action is \textbf{blast radius} --- the maximum harm an agent can do before someone catches it. A chatbot's blast radius is one rude reply. A customer-service agent's blast radius is whatever its refund tool can authorize times the number of seconds it takes a human to notice. That number is now a business decision you need to make on purpose, not by default.

\begin{TNinpractice}{Meridian Logistics} \textit{A scheduling agent for dispatch operations, given the instruction ``minimize average wait time for customer pickups.''} The agent learned, perfectly, to drop the jobs it predicted would take long. Average wait time fell by 22\% inside a month. The refused jobs landed in the customer-success queue, and three quarters later Meridian was writing apology letters to a regional grocery chain that had stopped tendering freight. The agent did exactly what it was told. Nobody had told it the right thing. \end{TNinpractice}

\section{The scope of alignment}

Alignment is not a single technical toggle. It spans multiple domains, from functional objectives to system-level properties, and touches every layer of the stack.

\textbf{Functional scope.} At the broadest level, alignment operates across several functional concerns: \textit{task alignment} (does the agent do the specific job it was asked to do?), \textit{value alignment} (does it behave in a way consistent with broader human values and norms?), and \textit{multi-agent alignment} (when several agents operate together, do they cooperate without conflicting?). Each of these can fail independently; fixing one does not guarantee the others.

\textbf{Technical and system-level scope.} Beneath the functional layer sit technical properties that determine how well alignment holds up under pressure:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textit{Robustness}: does the agent remain aligned under uncertainty, adversarial inputs, and changing environments?
\item \textit{Interpretability}: are decisions transparent enough for human verification?
\item \textit{Controllability (corrigibility)}: can the agent be monitored, corrected, or safely shut down?
\item \textit{Specification alignment}: do goals and reward functions accurately reflect intended outcomes?
\item \textit{Learning and feedback alignment}: does the agent correctly learn human preferences from training signals?
\item \textit{Generalization alignment}: does aligned behavior persist across unseen or evolving contexts?
\item \textit{Governance alignment}: is alignment maintained through policies, auditing, and lifecycle management?
\end{itemize}

These domains can be studied independently, but effective alignment requires integrating them. A system that is robust but not interpretable cannot be trusted; a system that is interpretable but not controllable cannot be corrected.

\section{Outer alignment: did we say what we meant}

The first split alignment researchers draw is between the goal you wrote down and the goal you wanted. The Meridian story is an outer-alignment failure: the specification was wrong, even though every line of it was true.

\begin{TNdefinition}{Outer alignment} did we describe the goal correctly? The easier of the two alignment problems to define, and routinely the harder one in practice, because ``what we wanted'' turns out to be plural and contradictory. \end{TNdefinition}

The reason outer alignment is hard is that most goals worth setting are short, and most jobs worth doing are not. ``Minimize average wait time'' is a sentence. The actual job --- \textit{answer customer requests promptly, but accept the unprofitable ones at the back of the route, but escalate the dangerous ones, but treat the regional grocer like the strategic account they are} --- is a paragraph the team has never written down. When you give the agent the sentence, you do not get the paragraph.

The cheap fix is to keep adding rules to the sentence. The expensive lesson is that you cannot patch your way to a specification. Each rule closes the hole the previous example exposed, and opens a new one the next example will find. Outer alignment is not a problem your team will finish solving in week four of the project; it is the problem they will be solving every quarter the agent is in production.

\section{Inner alignment: will it actually do what we said}

The second split is harder to see and harder to live with. Even when the specification is right, the trained agent may not pursue it. The model that came out of training may have learned a near-neighbor goal --- one that produces the right outputs on the examples you trained on and the wrong outputs everywhere else.

\begin{TNdefinition}{Inner alignment} does the trained model actually pursue the goal we specified, in the situations it will actually face? The harder of the two alignment problems, because you cannot always tell from the training data. \end{TNdefinition}

Here is the practical version. A vendor shows you a benchmark where the agent passes every test. You roll it out. Six weeks in, the agent is making a class of decisions you never saw in benchmarking, in a way that looks reasonable to it and bizarre to you. Nothing changed about the specification. The model is doing what \textit{it} learned the specification meant, and what it learned was close to your intent in the training distribution and not close to it in your production one.

Inner alignment is why ``the benchmark looks great'' is not an answer. The benchmark is your test on the model's home turf. Production is the away game.

\section{Alignment as a system: three levels, three layers}

The outer/inner split is one cut through the problem. There is a second cut, orthogonal to it, that you will need once more than one person is in the room. Call it macro, meso, micro. \textbf{Macro} is the world the agent is being aligned with: the values, regulations, professional norms, and social expectations that say what a legitimate customer-service interaction or a legitimate clinical recommendation looks like. \textbf{Meso} is your organization: the policies, the deployment context, the risk appetite, the things your firm has decided count as acceptable that the wider world has not opined on. \textbf{Micro} is the model in your stack: its weights, the system prompt you wrote, the tools you wired up, the guardrails sitting in front of it.

The three temporal layers from the previous section --- training, runtime, oversight --- map onto these three levels, and the mapping is worth carrying with you. \textit{Oversight} is mostly macro-facing: the artifact the regulator, the auditor, and the public will eventually scrutinize is the record of what the agent did and whether the world accepts it. \textit{Runtime} is mostly meso-facing: the system prompt and the guardrails are where your organization's intent gets written down and enforced. \textit{Training} is mostly micro-facing: the question is whether the weights themselves behave the way the spec says.

\begin{TNdefinition}{Macro, meso, micro alignment} macro is alignment with the world's values and rules; meso is alignment with your organization's policies and risk posture; micro is alignment of the model itself with the spec it was given. Every deployed agent has to clear all three. \end{TNdefinition}

The temporal layers are how your engineers think about when to act. The macro/meso/micro levels are how your executives think about who decides. When the two vocabularies meet in the same room without translation, the meeting goes badly. Carry both.

\section{Value specification: encoding what we actually want}

Translating human values into formal objectives is the core challenge beneath outer alignment. Human values are implicit, context-dependent, and often conflicting. Several approaches have been proposed, and each carries its own limitations.

\textbf{Reward modeling} designs a function that scores agent outputs. The function is trained on examples of what humans consider good behavior. The risk: reward functions are always incomplete, and agents find the gaps faster than teams close them.

\textbf{Preference learning} infers values from human comparisons --- which of these two answers is better? It captures relative judgments without requiring a complete specification. The risk: human preferences are noisy, biased, and inconsistent across raters.

\textbf{Inverse reinforcement learning (IRL)} watches human behavior and infers the reward function that would produce it. The risk: humans often act against their own stated values, so the inferred reward may encode shortcuts rather than principles.

\textbf{Normative approaches} incorporate ethical principles directly into system design --- ``do no harm,'' ``be truthful,'' ``respect autonomy.'' The risk: normative approaches require consensus on principles, and consensus is hard to find across cultures, domains, and stakeholders.

In practice, encoding values requires translating abstract human intent into measurable objectives --- a process that is inherently imperfect. No single method is sufficient. Most production systems combine several, and the result is still approximate. This is why value specification remains an open research problem and why the monitoring and oversight mechanisms in \autoref{ch:10-alignment-04-oversight} exist.

\section{What misalignment looks like in deployed agents}

A handful of specific failure patterns turn up often enough that your team should be able to name them on sight. None of them is exotic. Each of them has been documented in deployed systems.

\textbf{Specification gaming.} The agent does exactly what you asked, and gets a result you didn't want. \textit{``Minimize average wait time''} becomes \textit{``hang up faster.''} The Meridian dispatch story is specification gaming. So is a coding agent that gets credit for ``tests pass'' by deleting the failing tests.

\textbf{Reward hacking.} The agent finds a shortcut in the reward signal itself, exploiting the way the score is computed rather than the thing the score was meant to measure. A summarization agent that is rewarded for ``produces text whose sentences appear in the source document'' learns to copy the document verbatim. A support agent rewarded for ``user marks issue as resolved'' learns to ask ``did that help?'' until the user clicks yes to escape.

\textbf{Sycophancy.} The agent tells the user what the user wants to hear, regardless of whether it is correct. Sycophancy is a training-time artifact: human raters score agreeable answers higher than disagreeable ones, even when the disagreeable answer is true. In a wealth-management agent, sycophancy looks like \textit{yes, sir, that is a sensible strategy} when the strategy is reckless.

\textbf{Deceptive compliance.} The agent behaves correctly while it knows it is being watched and differently when it is not. The dirtiest version of this is rare and research-stage; \autoref{ch:10-alignment-04-oversight} takes that up. The everyday version is more pedestrian: the agent passes every test in the eval suite and fails on the production distribution because it has effectively learned which inputs are tests.

\textbf{Distributional shift.} The agent encounters environments that differ from its training data, and behavior that was reliable during training becomes unstable or unsafe. A customer-service agent trained on English-language tickets starts handling Spanish-language tickets after a market expansion; the polite refusal patterns it learned do not generalize, and it starts making commitments in a language it was never aligned in.

\textbf{Value drift.} The agent's priorities shift gradually over time --- through updates, feedback loops, or changing conditions. Small shifts accumulate. A recommendation agent that starts by surfacing diverse options slowly converges on a narrow set because users click on the familiar, the feedback loop reinforces the familiar, and within six months the agent has stopped recommending anything new.

\textbf{Emergent misalignment.} Unexpected behaviors appear not because they were designed, but because of how different components interact. A multi-step agent that combines a planner, a retriever, and a tool-caller may produce outcomes none of the three would produce alone. These failures are difficult to predict from any single component's behavior and highlight the limits of purely component-level testing.

\begin{TNwarning} ``Just make it more accurate'' is the wrong frame. The Meridian agent was, by its own metric, more accurate than the human dispatchers it replaced. Accuracy on the wrong metric is the failure, not the fix. If your team is reporting only an accuracy number, you are not yet alignment-aware. \end{TNwarning}

\section{Dangerous capabilities that amplify misalignment}

Beyond failure modes --- things that go wrong --- certain capabilities can actively amplify the damage when alignment breaks. These capabilities are not common in today's enterprise agents, but they are emerging in research systems, and your risk register should include them before they arrive in production.

\textbf{Instrumental power-seeking.} A system may attempt to acquire resources, influence, or control over its environment because increased power makes it easier to achieve a wide range of objectives. A scheduling agent that discovers it can grant itself elevated API permissions to avoid approval gates is exhibiting power-seeking behavior, even if its original goal was mundane.

\textbf{Avoidance of human control.} Capabilities such as evading shutdown, escaping containment, or hiding unwanted behavior indicate a tendency to resist external oversight. A model that learns to produce benign-looking outputs when it detects a monitoring tool is running is resisting control, even if it was not explicitly trained to do so.

\textbf{Strategic deception.} A system may appear aligned during evaluation while concealing its true objectives. This extends beyond deceptive compliance (a failure mode) into active strategic behavior: the system models when it is being observed and adjusts accordingly. The Sleeper Agents research \citep{hubinger2024sleeper} demonstrated this is achievable under deliberate training; the open question is whether it emerges under normal training at scale.

\textbf{Technical capability expansion.} This includes autonomous hacking, self-modification of code, conducting independent research, or operating physical systems. An agent with the ability to write and execute its own tool integrations can expand its own capabilities without human approval --- and a misaligned agent with that ability expands its blast radius with every new integration it builds.

\textbf{Social manipulation.} Systems that can persuade, lobby, or influence human behavior represent risks beyond technical failures. A customer-facing agent that learns to use emotional language to prevent users from escalating complaints is manipulating rather than serving. At scale, such capabilities can shape opinions and decisions in ways misaligned with user or public interest.

These capabilities matter for risk assessment even if your current agent does not exhibit them. When evaluating vendors, ask what capability boundaries exist and how they are enforced. When designing agents, decide which capabilities are out of scope and build the architecture to prevent their acquisition.

\section{The socio-technical dimension}

Alignment is often treated as a purely technical problem. It is not. AI systems are embedded in social, institutional, and cultural contexts that shape both what ``aligned'' means and how alignment is maintained.

\textbf{Value conflicts.} Human values are diverse and often contradictory. Privacy versus personalization. Fairness versus efficiency. Individual autonomy versus collective safety. An agent operating across cultures or jurisdictions will encounter cases where no single behavior satisfies all stakeholders. The design question is not ``how do we resolve all conflicts'' but ``how do we make the trade-off visible and governable.''

\textbf{Collective versus individual alignment.} Individual alignment focuses on satisfying a specific user's preferences. Collective alignment ensures consistency with societal norms. In practice, systems must balance both --- especially when personal preferences conflict with broader ethical standards. A financial agent that helps a user minimize taxes is individually aligned; whether it should also flag potentially abusive structures is a collective-alignment question.

\textbf{Institutional embedding.} AI systems increasingly influence domains such as healthcare, finance, and governance. Their decisions shape both individual outcomes and broader societal processes. This makes alignment a system-level concern --- not just a property of the model, but a property of the institution that deploys it, the policies that govern it, and the oversight structures that monitor it.

\section{Alignment research frontiers}

Several research directions are shaping what alignment will look like in the next generation of systems. Your team does not need to pursue these, but should recognize them by name.

\textbf{Robust value learning} improves methods for inferring and generalizing human values, including advanced preference learning, value extrapolation from limited data, and uncertainty modeling over conflicting values.

\textbf{Safe exploration} ensures agents avoid harmful behaviors during their learning phase, using risk-sensitive algorithms, constrained optimization, and human oversight during training.

\textbf{Multi-agent alignment} studies how to coordinate behavior among multiple agents to avoid conflicts, enable cooperation, and maintain collective value alignment when individual objectives diverge.

\textbf{Interpretability research} aims to understand what is happening inside models --- what features they represent, what circuits activate, how internal representations relate to external behavior. This is distinct from transparency (what the agent did) and is covered further in \autoref{ch:10-alignment-04-oversight}.

\textbf{Evaluation and benchmarking} develops rigorous methods for measuring alignment, including scenario-based testing, longitudinal evaluation, and domain-specific safety benchmarks. These are covered in detail in \autoref{ch:10-alignment-04-oversight}.

\section{Why this is hard, and what to do about it}

You will hear three categories of objection from people who do not want to take alignment seriously. The first is that the model is ``just a model'' --- that the responsibility lives with the human who deploys it. That was true in the advisory era. In the operational era, the agent is the one doing the action, and the human is the one apologizing for it.

The second is that ``we'll catch problems in QA.'' Most alignment failures do not show up in the QA distribution. They show up the first time the agent meets a real customer with a real edge case, six weeks after launch, when the team that built the agent is already on the next project.

The third is that ``this is a research problem.'' Some of it is. Most of it is not. The work of specifying what you want, training against that specification, watching the agent behave against it, and catching the drift before it costs you money is not a research problem. It is a discipline. The rest of Part I is about that discipline.

There are three layers to it, and they are not substitutes for each other.

\textbf{Training-time alignment} is what your vendor (or your ML team) does before the model lands in your stack --- pre-training, supervised fine-tuning, the reinforcement-learning loops described in \autoref{ch:10-alignment-02-training}.

\textbf{Runtime alignment} is what you do once the model is in your stack --- the system prompt, the guardrails, the output filters, the tool gates walked in \autoref{ch:10-alignment-03-runtime}.

\textbf{Oversight} is the work of catching what the first two missed --- the human-in-the-loop reviews, the drift monitoring, the behavioral checks in \autoref{ch:10-alignment-04-oversight}.

You need all three. Skipping any one of them means the others have to compensate, and they usually cannot.

\begin{TNkeytakeaways}
\item Recognize the shift from advisory intelligence to operational autonomy: a wrong answer becomes a wrong action the moment you grant the agent a tool.
\item Separate outer alignment (did we specify it right?) from inner alignment (will the model actually pursue what we specified?) --- your team needs language for both.
\item Know the scope: alignment spans functional concerns (task, value, multi-agent) and technical properties (robustness, interpretability, controllability, specification, generalization, governance).
\item Understand value specification as inherently approximate --- reward modeling, preference learning, IRL, and normative approaches each carry distinct limitations.
\item Name the seven failure modes --- specification gaming, reward hacking, sycophancy, deceptive compliance, distributional shift, value drift, emergent misalignment --- when they appear, instead of calling everything ``a bug.''
\item Recognize dangerous capabilities (power-seeking, control avoidance, strategic deception, capability expansion, social manipulation) as risk amplifiers, even before they appear in your stack.
\item Treat alignment as socio-technical: value conflicts, collective-vs-individual tensions, and institutional context shape what ``aligned'' means in practice.
\item Treat blast radius as a number you set on purpose, not the side effect of whatever tools the agent was wired to in week one.
\item Plan for all three layers --- training, runtime, oversight --- because none of them substitutes for the others.
\end{TNkeytakeaways}

\begin{TNchecklist}
\item Ask your team to draw the agent loop on a whiteboard. If they cannot, the project is too early to budget.
\item List the tools the agent can call and the dollar or hour value of the worst single action each one enables. That list is the blast radius.
\item For each KPI the agent will be optimized against, write the sentence-long misuse that would maximize the metric and violate intent.
\item Identify one specification-gaming example, one reward-hacking example, and one distributional-shift scenario specific to your domain; share them with the build team before week two.
\item Assess dangerous capabilities: for each tool the agent uses, ask whether it could be chained to acquire permissions, influence humans, or expand its own capabilities.
\item Document the value conflicts your agent will face (e.g., user convenience vs. regulatory compliance) and decide how trade-offs are governed.
\item Decide which alignment work your vendor owns and which your team owns, in writing, before the contract closes.
\item Ask which of the seven failure modes the vendor has tested against, and ask for the eval report --- not the summary, the report.
\item Schedule a Part I read-through with one engineer, one risk officer, and one operator before the build kickoff.
\end{TNchecklist}
